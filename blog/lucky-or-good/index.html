Lucky or good? Testing a tuned strategy for luck

Try enough settings and keep the best one, and its result looks better than it is. Here is how we measure how much better, and how we turn that into a go or no-go call.

November 16, 2026 · Slava Boichenko

When we tune a trading strategy, the moment I trust least is the one that feels best: the search finishes, and one setting is clearly ahead of the rest. We built the checks in this post so that this moment always comes with a second opinion.

Here is why it needs one. Ask 100 people to flip a fair coin 20 times each. On average they get 10 heads, but the luckiest of the hundred will usually get 15 or 16. Nobody in the room is good at flipping coins; that is simply what the best of 100 random results looks like. Hear only about the winner, though, and 15 heads out of 20 sounds like a talent.

Tuning works the same way. A strategy has settings, such as entry thresholds, holding times and exit rules. We replay recorded market data once for each candidate setting and keep the one that made the most money. That winner's result is the maximum of many noisy results, so it is biased upward: part of its good week was noise that happened to suit its thresholds, and that part will not come back. This is the selection effect, the inflation you get when you pick the best of many tries and look only at that one.

So before a tuned version replaces the one we run, we ask: given how many settings we tried, is the winner's result too good to be luck? Below are the tests we use and how they combine into one decision. Every number is illustrative, chosen to show how the arithmetic behaves; none is a result of ours.

The short version. Before a tuned setting replaces the current one, we check five things:

  1. How many independent trades the data is really worth.
  2. How high the best of N settings would score with no edge at all, and how likely the winner is to beat that.
  3. Whether its entries beat random entries that use the same exits.
  4. Whether it still makes money if every trade pays 25% more in costs.
  5. How often the best setting on half the days flops on the other half (advisory).

The answers combine, by rules fixed in advance, into one of four outcomes: RED do not adopt; AMBER adopt, and watch the weak spots; GREEN adopt; NO ANSWER a test could not compute, so we report it and do not block.

The example we will follow

We will carry one made-up case through every step. You tried 100 settings on one week of data, and the winner made 280 trades with a per-trade Sharpe ratio of 0.20, a skewness of 1.5 and a kurtosis of 8 (step two explains the last two). Should you switch to it?

What counts as a trade, and a return

Each replay produces a list of trades with entry and exit times, prices, fees and slippage, the gap between the price you expected when you decided to trade and the price you actually got. We keep a trade only if it falls inside the test period and does not overlap a gap in our data, because during a gap the replay could not see the market. Each remaining trade becomes a return, its net profit divided by the stake, and the Sharpe ratio is the average return divided by its standard deviation:

SR^=rˉsr\widehat{\mathrm{SR}} = \dfrac{\bar{r}}{s_r}SR=sr​rˉ​
The per-trade Sharpe ratio. The standard deviation uses n − 1, and nothing is annualized.

Think of it as average profit in units of wobble. Our example's 0.20 means the average trade earns a fifth of a typical trade's swing. That sounds small, but per trade it is a solid edge: a few hundred trades at 0.20 rarely end up negative overall, while a few hundred at 0.02 often do.

Step one: how many independent trades do we really have?

How much evidence does the week actually hold? Every later test grows more confident as the trade count grows, so an inflated count makes all of them overconfident. And 280 trades are not 280 independent pieces of evidence. Trades on the same day share a market mood: on a hot day most entries win, and on a dead day most lose. If that day effect is strong, 280 trades carry closer to a handful of days' worth of information than 280 coin flips' worth.

We measure the day effect with the intraclass correlation ρ, the share of the spread in returns explained by which day a trade fell on. At 0 the day does not matter; at 1 all the trades on a day behave like a single trade. Negative estimates are set to 0, written ρ+.

The math behind ρ

We use the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) estimate:

Between-day mean square: how far each day's average return sits from the overall average, weighted by trades.
MSW

The number of days, the number of trades on day i, and the total number of trades.

Kish's design effect (Kish, 1965) then turns ρ into an effective sample size, the number of independent trades the week is worth:

Neff=n1+(m−1) ρ+N_{\mathrm{eff}} = \dfrac{n}{1+(m-1)\,\rho^{+}}Neff​=1+(m−1)ρ+n​
Here n is the number of trades and m the typical day size, weighted toward busy days. The more uneven the days, the larger m, and the harder the same ρ bites.
The math behind m

When days are uneven, one busy day dominates, so instead of the plain average day size we use Kish's cluster size, where di is the number of trades on day i:

With equal days, m is just n divided by the number of days.

Even a small day effect (ρ = 0.05) cuts 280 trades to about 95 effective ones, and to 29 if one day dominates. For our example, say the trades are even, 40 a day, and the day effect is a little milder, about 0.034, which leaves 120 effective trades. That number, not 280, goes into the luck test, and we flag any result that rests on too few effective trades. We also report an interval for ρ itself, found by resampling whole days.

Days are not the only cluster. Several trades in the same asset, say a burst of entries into one name during one move, share a fate too, often more than trades that merely share a day. Methods exist for clustering two ways at once (Cameron, Gelbach and Miller, 2011), but ours corrects for days only, so we read Neff as an upper bound and treat a strategy that keeps returning to the same few assets with extra caution.

Step two: the Deflated Sharpe Ratio

Now the coin-flip question: is the winner's Sharpe higher than the best of 100 settings with no edge would be? The Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR) of Bailey and López de Prado (2014) answers it in two steps.

How high would luck alone reach?

Suppose none of the settings had any real edge, so every true Sharpe is 0 and every measured Sharpe is noise. Extreme-value theory, the branch of statistics that deals with the largest and smallest values in a sample, gives a good estimate of the largest Sharpe you would expect among N such settings. We call it the luck bar:

SR0=V⋅[(1−γE) Φ−1 ⁣(1−1N)+γE Φ−1 ⁣(1−1Ne)]\mathrm{SR}_0 = \sqrt{V}\cdot\left[(1-\gamma_E)\,\Phi^{-1}\!\left(1-\dfrac{1}{N}\right)+\gamma_E\,\Phi^{-1}\!\left(1-\dfrac{1}{N e}\right)\right]SR0​=V​⋅[(1−γE​)Φ−1(1−N1​)+γE​Φ−1(1−Ne1​)]
The expected maximum Sharpe ratio of N unskilled trials.

V is the variance of the Sharpe ratios across the settings tried, and N is the number of independent settings, which we do not know exactly (more below). The more the settings disagree, the higher luck can lift the best one; each extra setting is one more lottery ticket, though the bar grows only slowly with N.

How sure are we that the winner clears the bar?

The winner's measured Sharpe is itself noisy. The DSR is the probability that its true Sharpe is above the luck bar, given the effective number of trades and how oddly shaped the returns are:

DSR=Φ ⁣((SR^−SR0)T−11−γ3 SR^+γ4−14 SR^2)\mathrm{DSR} = \Phi\!\left(\dfrac{\left(\widehat{\mathrm{SR}}-\mathrm{SR}_0\right)\sqrt{T-1}}{\sqrt{1-\gamma_3\,\widehat{\mathrm{SR}}+\dfrac{\gamma_4-1}{4}\,\widehat{\mathrm{SR}}^{2}}}\right)DSR=Φ​1−γ3​SR+4γ4​−1​SR2​(SR−SR0​)T−1​​​
Φ turns a z-score into a probability between 0 and 1. A DSR of 0.95 means roughly 95% confidence that the edge beats what luck alone would show.

The top is the lead over the bar, scaled up by the evidence: T is the effective trade count from step one. The bottom corrects for shape. A few big wins (positive skewness γ3) help a little, while fat tails (high kurtosis γ4) make the Sharpe less reliable and pull the DSR down.

The symbols in the DSR
SR^−SR0\widehat{\mathrm{SR}}-\mathrm{SR}_0SR−SR0​
How far the winner sits above the luck bar. If it is below the bar, the DSR is under 0.5.

More evidence, more confidence. T is the effective number of trades from step one, not the raw count, so correlated days cannot inflate it.
γ3 (skewness)
Trading returns are often lopsided. Many small losses and a few big wins is positive skew, which shrinks the denominator and helps a little. Many small wins and rare crashes is negative skew, which hurts.
γ4 (kurtosis)
How fat the tails are; a normal curve has 3. With fat tails a handful of extreme trades drive the average, so the Sharpe is less reliable. That raises the denominator and lowers the DSR.

For our example: a lead of 0.073, times √119 ≈ 10.9, divided by the shape term √0.77 ≈ 0.88, gives z ≈ 0.91 and a DSR of 0.82, about an 82% chance that the true Sharpe beats the bar.

We do not know how many settings we tried

N should count only genuinely different settings, and that is hard to pin down. Close neighbors of one setting are not independent tries, yet earlier research rounds, even forgotten ones, add to the selection effect. So we compute the DSR at a low, a middle and a high assumed count. The middle one is the gate, a check that can block the switch on its own; the other two show how fragile the conclusion is. The code: effective trades, luck bar and DSR in Python

The formulas above in plain Python, standard library only. Feed it per-trade returns grouped by day, the spread of your trial Sharpes and your number of settings. It is a sketch: it skips the zero-variance and too-few-settings checks listed above. Run as a script, it prints four numbers from the tables on three lines: 95, 29, then 0.127 and 0.82. """Effective sample size, luck bar and Deflated Sharpe Ratio, as in the post.
Standard library only (Python 3.8+). Returns are per trade: net profit / stake."""
from math import e, sqrt
from statistics import NormalDist, fmean, stdev

PHI = NormalDist()
EULER_GAMMA = 0.5772156649015329


def icc(days):
    """One-way ANOVA intraclass correlation; days = one list of returns per day."""
    days = [d for d in days if d]
    sizes = [len(d) for d in days]
    n, k = sum(sizes), len(days)
    if k < 2 or n <= k:
        return 0.0
    grand = fmean(x for d in days for x in d)
    msb = sum(len(d) * (fmean(d) - grand) ** 2 for d in days) / (k - 1)
    msw = sum((x - fmean(d)) ** 2 for d in days for x in d) / (n - k)
    n0 = (n - sum(s * s for s in sizes) / n) / (k - 1)
    den = msb + (n0 - 1) * msw
    return max(0.0, (msb - msw) / den) if den > 0 else 0.0


def kish_n_eff(sizes, rho):
    """Effective trades from the day sizes and rho+, using Kish's cluster size m."""
    n = sum(sizes)
    m = sum(s * s for s in sizes) / n
    return n / (1 + (m - 1) * max(rho, 0.0))


def luck_bar(sd_trial_sharpes, n_trials):
    """SR0: the best Sharpe expected among n_trials settings with no edge."""
    q = PHI.inv_cdf
    return sd_trial_sharpes * ((1 - EULER_GAMMA) * q(1 - 1 / n_trials)
                               + EULER_GAMMA * q(1 - 1 / (n_trials * e)))


def sharpe_shape(returns):
    """Per-trade Sharpe (n - 1), population skewness, non-excess kurtosis."""
    mu, n = fmean(returns), len(returns)
    m2, m3, m4 = (sum((x - mu) ** p for x in returns) / n for p in (2, 3, 4))
    return mu / stdev(returns), m3 / m2 ** 1.5, m4 / m2 ** 2


def dsr(sr, sr0, t, skew, kurt):
    """P(true Sharpe > SR0) with T effective trades; None means 'no answer'."""
    rad = 1 - skew * sr + (kurt - 1) / 4 * sr * sr
    if t <= 1 or rad <= 0:
        return None
    return PHI.cdf((sr - sr0) * sqrt(t - 1) / sqrt(rad))


if __name__ == "__main__":
    print(round(kish_n_eff([40] * 7, 0.05)))              # 95, as in the first table
    print(round(kish_n_eff([10] * 6 + [220], 0.05)))      # 29
    bar = luck_bar(0.05, 100)
    print(round(bar, 3), round(dsr(0.20, bar, 120, 1.5, 8), 2))  # 0.127 0.82

Step three: a placebo

Where does the edge come from? A strategy decides when to enter and when to exit, and tuning improves both. Good exits alone can make money from almost any entries, so we want to know whether the entry rules add anything.

So we race the winner against a placebo. The placebo enters at random, matched to the real strategy on the characteristics its entries could otherwise exploit. It uses exactly the winner's tuned exits, and it takes every random entry its plan gives it, so nothing clever filters it.

“Matched” carries most of the weight here. In thin, young markets, many assets at a random moment are close to dead: little liquidity left, few buyers, sometimes one large holder about to sell. A placebo that enters those blindly takes trades no real strategy would take, and the replay has to invent fills for it, at prices nobody would have paid or for more than the market could hold. It usually loses, so even a weak strategy beats it, and now and then an impossible fill hands it a windfall; either way, it says nothing about entry skill. So the random entries must come from the same kind of opportunity the strategy saw, matched on the liquidity at entry and on the asset's age, and every simulated fill must fit inside the liquidity that was actually there. We learned the second part the hard way: one of our own placebo runs booked a fill larger than the market it was trading in. A placebo that is too easy to beat is worse than none, because it turns a weak result into a confident one.

The only difference left is the entry rule. We measure profit per trade, strategy minus placebo:

Δ=∑netstrategynstrategy−∑netplacebonplacebo\Delta = \dfrac{\sum \mathrm{net}_{\mathrm{strategy}}}{n_{\mathrm{strategy}}} - \dfrac{\sum \mathrm{net}_{\mathrm{placebo}}}{n_{\mathrm{placebo}}}Δ=nstrategy​∑netstrategy​​−nplacebo​∑netplacebo​​
Average net profit per trade, strategy minus placebo.

Day-block bootstrap

We cannot rerun history, so the bootstrap simulates other periods by drawing days, with replacement, from the ones we have:

  1. Draw as many days as we have, with replacement, so a good day may appear twice and a bad day not at all. Do this separately for the strategy and the placebo.
  2. Recompute Δ from those days' trades.
  3. Repeat many thousands of times, with a fixed random seed so the result is reproducible.
  4. Sort the values. The 5th and 95th percentiles give a 90% interval for Δ.

We draw whole days because, as in step one, trades on one day move together; drawing single trades would give a falsely narrow interval. The entries pass if the lower end of the interval is above zero, that is, if they beat random entries even in the less favorable draws.

Mann-Whitney as a second opinion

One enormous trade can dominate an average, so we also run a test that uses only order. Mann-Whitney ranks all trades from both groups together and asks whether a random strategy trade tends to rank above a random placebo trade. Its answer is a p-value, the chance of a lead at least this large if the entries were really no better than random; below 0.05 counts as a pass.

The math behind the Mann-Whitney test
U=R1−n1(n1+1)2z=U−n1n2/2−12σUp=1−Φ(z)\begin{gathered}U = R_1 - \dfrac{n_1(n_1+1)}{2} \\[0.9em] z = \dfrac{U - n_1 n_2/2 - \tfrac{1}{2}}{\sigma_U} \\[0.9em] p = 1-\Phi(z)\end{gathered}U=R1​−2n1​(n1​+1)​z=σU​U−n1​n2​/2−21​​p=1−Φ(z)​
R1 is the sum of the strategy trades' ranks, and n1 and n2 are the trade counts of the strategy and the placebo. σU includes a correction for ties, and the −½ is a continuity correction. The test is one-sided: we only care whether the strategy is better.

Failing either placebo test does not sink a result by itself; it raises a caution flag. In our example, this is where we would learn whether the 0.20 comes from the entries or just from good exits.

Step four: cost stress

Would the edge survive if trading cost more than the replay assumed? Replays charge each trade the fees and slippage our cost model measures, but real costs can come in higher, and thin edges vanish quickly when they do. So we charge every trade again at a multiple k of its measured costs:

neti(k)=neti−(k−1)⋅(feesi+slippagei)k∈{1.25, 1.5, 2}\begin{gathered}\mathrm{net}_i^{(k)} = \mathrm{net}_i - (k-1)\cdot\left(\mathrm{fees}_i + \mathrm{slippage}_i\right) \\[0.9em] k \in \{1.25,\, 1.5,\, 2\}\end{gathered}neti(k)​=neti​−(k−1)⋅(feesi​+slippagei​)k∈{1.25,1.5,2}​

At k = 1.25 each trade pays 25% more than its measured costs, and that level is the gate: if the average net profit per trade there is zero or below, the verdict is red. We also report 1.5× and 2× to show how much headroom is left, but they decide nothing. The test is applied after the fact, without re-simulating which trades would still have filled, and it stresses only the costs we measure, not the delay between a decision and its execution.

Step five: probability of backtest overfitting (advisory)

The last check asks whether “best in tuning” predicts “good on new data” at all: had we tuned on some days and tested on the rest, how often would the best setting have done worse than a typical one on the days it had not seen? Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado and Zhu (2017) answer it with combinatorially symmetric cross-validation (CSCV):

  1. Split the days into two halves in every possible way, one for tuning and one for testing. With 7 days, one half gets 3 days and the other 4, which gives 35 splits; the original method uses an even number of equal blocks, and this is our small variant for an odd count.
  2. For each split, find the setting with the highest total on the tuning half.
  3. Rank that setting among all K settings on the test half (1 = worst).
  4. Turn the rank into a relative position ω between 0 and 1, and that into its log-odds λ, the logarithm of ω/(1 − ω), which is negative when the setting landed in the bottom half. The probability of backtest overfitting (PBO) is the share of splits where that happened.
The math behind the PBO estimate
ω=rankK+1λ=ln⁡ω1−ωPBO=#{splits with λ≤0}# splits\begin{gathered}\omega = \dfrac{\mathrm{rank}}{K+1} \\[0.9em] \lambda = \ln\dfrac{\omega}{1-\omega} \\[0.9em] \mathrm{PBO} = \dfrac{\#\{\text{splits with } \lambda \le 0\}}{\#\,\text{splits}}\end{gathered}ω=K+1rank​λ=ln1−ωω​PBO=#splits#{splits with λ≤0}​​

A PBO of 0.2 means the tuning champion fell into the bottom half in 20% of splits, which is reassuring. Above 0.5, “best in tuning” predicts “good out of sample” no better than a coin flip: classic overfitting. With few days and closely related settings it is noisy, so it can add a caution but never blocks a result on its own.

Putting it together: a verdict decided in advance

The results feed one decision with four possible outcomes: red, amber, green and no answer. The rules and thresholds are fixed before anyone looks at the data, because a gate you can adjust after seeing the numbers is just more tuning. The thresholds below are one example, a choice rather than a law:

VerdictExample ruleConsequence
REDDSR at the gate count below 0.80, or average net per trade at 1.25 times costs at most 0Do not adopt the tuned version
NO ANSWERThe DSR cannot be computed and the cost stress is not redReport it; do not block
GREENDSR at the gate count at or above 0.95, placebo interval above 0, still profitable at 1.25 times costs, and no caution flagsAdopt
AMBERAnything elseAdopt, with the weak spots listed and watched

Any one caution flag turns a result that is not red into amber: a DSR between 0.80 and 0.95; a placebo interval that includes zero; a Mann-Whitney p-value of 0.05 or more; a PBO above 0.5, or one that cannot be computed; or fewer effective trades than a minimum set in advance.

The design is lopsided on purpose. Red is reserved for two claims: the result is probably luck, or costs eat the edge. Amber means “go ahead, and here is what to watch.” A test that cannot compute does not block, and neither does a tool that crashes or gets bad input, because a broken referee should not stop the game. The checks can therefore fail open, and we accept that because the real safeguard comes after them: the forward test on new data, described at the end.

Red is not a verdict on the idea behind a strategy either; often it only says the sample is too small. With the example bar of 0.127, a strategy whose true per-trade Sharpe is 0.15 and measures 0.15 scores a DSR of about 0.58 on 60 effective trades, which is red. To tell a true 0.15 from the 0.127 bar, with a DSR of 0.95 and an 80% chance of getting it, you need roughly nine thousand effective trades. So red starts a diagnosis: we look at the timing of entries, each exit rule, and costs and delays to learn whether the edge is missing, eaten by execution, or not yet measurable.

What this does not do

These tests catch one common failure, a lucky tuning winner. They do not prove an edge is real. One short sample is one market mood, and no amount of statistics extracts more from it than it contains; the effective sample size is an estimate, the settings count is a judgment call, and the cost model is a model.

What this means in practice

Back to our winner: 280 trades in a week at a per-trade Sharpe of 0.20. Step one made those 120 effective trades; step two put the luck bar at 0.127 and the DSR at 0.82, or 0.75 if we had really tried closer to 300 settings. That DSR is a caution flag on its own, so green is off the table. If the cost stress came out red, the verdict would be red; otherwise, whatever the placebo and the PBO say, it is amber.

The margin is thin. Had the day effect been 0.05, the same week would have counted as 95 effective trades, for a DSR of 0.79, just inside red. To pass the luck test outright at the same Sharpe, it would need about 390 effective trades, more than three times what this week gave.

So, amber: we adopt the tuned version, write down its weak spots (a modest DSR, a result that depends on the settings count, one week of data) and watch them. Red would have kept the current version and started a diagnosis; green would have adopted it with nothing in particular to watch; no answer would have been reported without blocking the switch.

None of these outcomes proves an edge; the forward test does. A forward test runs the tuned version, settings frozen, on market data it has never seen, as that data arrives, and judges it by rules written in advance. The checks above decide only whether a candidate deserves that trial. I think of them as a patient skeptic sitting next to the search: they can never tell us we are good, but they are very good at catching the times we were only lucky.
