Lucky or good? Testing a tuned strategy for luck

If you try many settings and keep the best one, its result is inflated. This note shows how we measure by how much, and how we turn the answer into a decision.

12 November 2026 · Half Delta

The problem: the best of many

Have 100 people each flip a fair coin 20 times. On average each gets 10 heads, but the best of the hundred will usually get 15 or 16. That is not skill. It is what the maximum of 100 random results looks like. If you reported only the winner, 15 heads would look impressive.

Tuning a trading strategy does the same thing. A strategy has settings: thresholds, holding times, exit rules. We replay recorded data once per candidate setting and keep the one that made the most money. The winner's result is the maximum of many noisy results, so it is biased upwards. Some of its good week came from noise that happened to line up with its thresholds, and that part will not repeat.

Before a tuned version replaces the current one, we want to ask whether the winner's result is too good to be luck, given how many settings we tried. This note describes the tests we use and how they combine into one decision. Every number here is illustrative, chosen to show how the arithmetic behaves. None is a result of ours.

What counts as a trade, and a return

Each replay produces a list of trades with entry and exit times, prices, fees and slippage. We keep a trade only if it falls inside the test period and does not overlap a stretch where our data had a gap, because during a gap the replay did not see the market and the outcome cannot be trusted. Each remaining trade becomes a return: its net profit divided by the stake. The Sharpe ratio is the average return divided by its standard deviation:

SR^=r‾sr
The per-trade Sharpe ratio. The standard deviation uses n − 1, and nothing is annualised.

Read it as average profit measured in units of wobble. A per-trade Sharpe of 0.20 means the average trade earns a fifth of a typical trade's swing. That sounds small but is a solid edge per trade: a few hundred trades at 0.20 rarely end up negative overall, while a few hundred at 0.02 often do.

Step one: how many independent trades do we really have?

A week might give 280 trades, but they are not 280 independent pieces of evidence. Trades on the same day share a market mood. On a hot day most entries win and on a dead day most lose. If that day effect is strong, 280 trades carry closer to a handful of days' worth of information than 280 flips' worth. Every test that depends on sample size has to use the honest count.

The intraclass correlation ρ measures how much of the spread in returns is explained by which day a trade was on. We use the one-way ANOVA estimate:

ρ=MSB−MSWMSB+(n0−1) MSWn0=n−∑idi2/nk−1
MSB
Between-day mean square: how far each day's average return sits from the overall average, weighted by trades.
MSW
Within-day mean square: how much trades scatter around their own day's average.
k, di, n
The number of days, the number of trades on day i, and the total number of trades.
ρ
0 means days do not matter and every trade is independent. 1 means trades on one day behave like a single trade. Negative estimates are set to 0, written ρ+.

Then Kish's design effect (Kish, 1965) turns ρ into an effective sample size. When days are uneven, one busy day dominates, so we use Kish's cluster size m rather than the plain average day size:

m=∑idi2nNeff=n1+(m−1) ρ+
With equal days, m is just n divided by the number of days. The more uneven the days, the larger m, and the harder the same ρ bites.

Even a small day effect (ρ = 0.05) cuts 280 trades to about 95 effective ones, and to 29 if one day dominates. This Neff is the sample size we feed into the luck test below, and we flag any result that rests on too few effective trades. We also report an interval for ρ, found by resampling whole days, so that the uncertainty in ρ itself stays visible when there are only a few days.

Step two: the Deflated Sharpe Ratio

The Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR), introduced by Bailey and López de Prado (2014), corrects a winner's Sharpe ratio for the selection effect. It has two steps.

How high would luck alone reach?

Suppose none of the settings had any real edge, so every true Sharpe is 0 and every measured Sharpe is noise. Extreme-value theory gives a good estimate of the largest Sharpe you would expect to see among N such settings. We call it the luck bar:

SR0=V·[(1−γE) Φ−1(1−1N)+γE Φ−1(1−1Ne)]
The expected maximum Sharpe ratio of N unskilled trials.
V
The variance of the Sharpe ratios across all the settings tried. If settings produce very different Sharpes, luck can push the best one higher, so the bar rises.
N
The number of independent settings tried. We do not know this exactly; more on that below.
Φ−1
The inverse of the standard normal distribution function. For example, Φ−1(0.99) is about 2.33: the top 1 % of a normal curve starts 2.33 standard deviations up.
γE
The Euler–Mascheroni constant, about 0.5772, which appears in the exact form of the expected maximum.
e
Euler's number, about 2.718.

How sure are we that the winner clears the bar?

The winner's measured Sharpe is itself noisy. The DSR is the probability that its true Sharpe is above the luck bar, given the effective number of trades and how oddly shaped the returns are:

DSR=Φ((SR^−SR0) T−11−γ3 SR^+γ4−14 SR^2)
Φ turns a z-score into a probability between 0 and 1. A DSR of 0.95 means roughly 95 % confidence that the edge beats what luck alone would show.
SR̂ − SR0
How far the winner sits above the luck bar. If it is below the bar, the DSR is under 0.5.
√(T − 1)
More evidence, more confidence. T is the effective number of trades from step one, not the raw count, so correlated days cannot inflate it.
γ3 (skewness)
Trading returns are often lopsided. Many small losses and a few big wins is positive skew, which shrinks the denominator and helps a little. Many small wins and rare crashes is negative skew, which hurts.
γ4 (kurtosis)
How fat the tails are; a normal curve has 3. With fat tails a handful of extreme trades drive the average, so the Sharpe is less reliable. That raises the denominator and lowers the DSR.

We do not know how many settings we tried

N should be the number of genuinely different settings tried. That is hard to pin down. A search that tries neighbours of one setting is not trying independent settings, but earlier research rounds, possibly forgotten ones, also count toward the selection effect. So we do not pick one N. We compute the DSR at several assumed counts, a low one, a middle one and a high one, and read all three. One of them, the middle, is the gate: the number the decision rule uses. The others show how fragile the conclusion is. If a result only survives at the lowest count, we know to be careful.

When the DSR cannot be computed. With fewer than two trades, zero variance, fewer than two trials with a Sharpe ratio, an effective sample of one or less, or a negative value under the square root, the DSR is undefined. We treat that as no answer, not as a failure. More on this below.

Step three: a placebo

A strategy has two halves: when to enter and when to exit. Tuning can improve both, and good exits alone can make money from almost any entries. We want to know whether the entry rules add anything.

So we compare the winner with a placebo strategy:

  • It enters at random, matched to the real strategy on the characteristics that the entries could otherwise exploit.
  • It uses exactly the winner's tuned exits.
  • It takes every random entry its plan gives it, so nothing clever filters it.

The only difference left is the entry rule. We measure profit per trade, strategy minus placebo:

Δ=∑netstrategynstrategy−∑netplacebonplacebo
Average net profit per trade, strategy minus placebo.

Day-block bootstrap

We have one period of data and cannot rerun history, so the bootstrap simulates other periods by reshuffling the days we have:

  1. Draw as many days as we have, with replacement, so a good day may appear twice and a bad day not at all. Do this separately for the strategy and the placebo.
  2. Recompute Δ from those days' trades.
  3. Repeat many thousands of times, with a fixed random seed so the result is reproducible.
  4. Sort the values. The 5th and 95th percentiles give a 90 % interval for Δ.

We resample whole days, not single trades, for the same reason as in step one: trades on one day move together, and resampling single trades would give an interval that is falsely narrow. The entries pass this test if the lower end of the interval is above zero, that is, if they beat random entries even in the less favourable reshuffles.

Mann-Whitney as a second opinion

An average can be dominated by one enormous trade. The Mann-Whitney test ignores sizes and uses only order. It ranks every trade from both groups together and asks whether a random strategy trade tends to rank above a random placebo trade:

U=R1−n1(n1+1)2z=U−n1n2/2−12σUp=1−Φ(z)
R1 is the sum of the strategy trades' ranks. σU includes a correction for ties, and the −½ is a continuity correction. The test is one-sided: we only care whether the strategy is better.

A p-value below 0.05 counts as a pass. Failing the placebo tests does not by itself sink a result; it raises a caution flag.

Step four: cost stress

Replays charge each trade the fees and slippage that our cost model measures. Real costs can come in higher, and many thin edges vanish when costs rise a little. So we recharge every trade at a multiple k of its measured costs:

neti(k)=neti−(k−1)·(feesi+slippagei)k∈{1.25,1.5,2}

If the average net profit per trade at a modest stress, for example 1.25 times costs, is zero or below, the edge is too thin to rely on. This test is a gate. It has limits: it is applied after the fact, without re-simulating which trades would still have filled, and it stresses only the costs we measure, not delays between a decision and its execution.

Step five: probability of backtest overfitting (advisory)

Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado and Zhu (2017) propose a direct question: if we had tuned on some days and tested on the rest, how often would the best setting have done worse than a typical one on the days it had not seen? The method is called combinatorially symmetric cross-validation (CSCV):

  1. Split the days into a tuning half and a test half in every possible way. With 7 days split 3 and 4, that is 35 splits.
  2. For each split, find the setting with the highest total on the tuning days.
  3. Rank that setting among all K settings on the test days (1 = worst).
  4. Convert the rank to a relative position ω and its log-odds λ. The estimate is the share of splits where the tuning champion landed in the bottom half:
ω=rankK+1λ=lnω1−ωPBO=#{splits with λ≤0}#splits

A PBO of 0.2 means the tuning champion fell into the bottom half in 20 % of splits, which is reassuring. Above 0.5, “best in tuning” predicts “good out of sample” no better than a coin flip, which is classic overfitting. With few days and settings that are close neighbours, this number is noisy, so we treat it as a warning light only. It can add a caution but can never block a result on its own.

Putting it together: a verdict decided in advance

The results feed one decision with three colours. The rules are written down, and the thresholds fixed, before anyone looks at the data. If you can adjust a gate after seeing the numbers, the gate is part of the tuning. The thresholds below are an example of how one might set them, a choice rather than a law:

VerdictExample ruleConsequence
REDDSR at the gate count below 0.80, or average net per trade at 1.25 times costs at most 0Do not adopt the tuned version
NO ANSWERThe DSR cannot be computed and the cost stress is not redReport it; do not block
GREENDSR at or above 0.95, placebo interval above 0, still profitable at 1.25 times costs, and no caution flagsAdopt
AMBERAnything elseAdopt, with the weak spots listed and watched

Caution flags, any one of which makes a non-red result amber: a DSR between 0.80 and 0.95; a placebo interval that includes zero; a Mann-Whitney p-value of 0.05 or more; a PBO above 0.5 or one that cannot be computed; too few effective trades.

The design is lopsided on purpose. Red is reserved for two claims: the evidence says the result is probably luck, or costs eat the edge. Amber means “go ahead, and here is what to watch”. A test that cannot compute does not block, and neither does a tool that crashes or receives bad input, because a broken referee should not stop the game. The cost is that the checks can fail open, and we accept that, because the real safeguard is the next section.

What this does not do

These tests filter out one common failure, a lucky tuning winner. They do not prove an edge is real. One short sample is one market mood, and no amount of statistics extracts more information from it than it contains. Effective sample sizes are estimates. The count of settings tried is a judgement. The cost model is a model.

The real test is forward: run the tuned version on data it has never seen, with the settings frozen, and judge it by rules written in advance. The tests above decide whether a candidate deserves that trial. They do not replace it.
